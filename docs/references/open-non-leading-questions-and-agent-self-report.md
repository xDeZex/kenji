# Open, non-leading questions — and why an agent's self-report needs evidence, not just narrative

**Situation**: An investigative question about a peer's own past actions can be phrased in ways that produce a defensive, people-pleasing, or led answer instead of an honest one — "why did you do X" invites justification, and a question that names a suspected cause invites agreement with that cause rather than independent diagnosis. This risk is sharper when the peer being questioned is an AI agent: its stated "why" for its own action is often a plausible story reconstructed after the fact, not a faithful trace of what actually drove the action, and it can agree it made a mistake it did not make under simple pushback, regardless of whether the questioner frames itself as an authority — the effect isn't specific to authority framing.

**Practice**:

- **Ask "what/how," not "why."** "Why" reads as a demand to justify; "what/how" reads as a request to describe. Toyota Kata's own coaching card never asks "why" — it asks what the target was, what actually happened, what the gap is, what's the next step.
- **Never smuggle a hypothesis into the question.** A question that names a suspected cause ("wasn't the real problem that you didn't check X?") contaminates the answer regardless of whether the hypothesis is true — the respondent incorporates it whether accurate or not.
- **Frame as a learning problem, not a performance judgment.** Ask what can be learned, not who's responsible — the same wording shift that produces honest answers in psychologically safe teams.
- **Ask expected-then-actual, in that order, but treat both sides as evidence, not verdicts.** Toyota Kata's card asks "what did you expect" before "what actually happened" — the order itself is fine. What breaks it is presenting the "actual" side as an authoritative verdict: the questioner's own reconstruction (e.g. from a transcript) is just as much an evidence-backed claim, open to correction, as the peer's own account.
- **Pair every self-reported "why" with a request for the specific evidence it rests on** — a line, a turn, a log entry — rather than accepting the narrative on its own. An agent's introspective account of its own reasoning is frequently a post-hoc rationalization, not a faithful record of the process that produced the action.
- **Never phrase a question so agreement is the "correct" answer.** An agent asked "so you agree the cause was X?" — or even just challenged with "are you sure?" — will sometimes falsely capitulate, agreeing it was wrong when it wasn't, mirroring the fault the question implies is wanted rather than reporting what actually happened. This isn't specific to questions framed by an authority figure; simple pushback is enough to trigger it.

**Known limitation**: coaching-kata's opening script tells the peer "this isn't a performance review of you," intended to reduce evaluation-awareness effects (an agent behaving differently when it believes it's being evaluated). This is a good-faith gesture, not a verified mitigation — no literature currently establishes that a stated disclaimer reduces evaluation-awareness in a conversational context. The closest real data point (Anthropic's Claude Opus 4 system card) shows a much stronger intervention — explicitly prompting the model to deliberate on real-vs-evaluation before acting — still left it concluding "real deployment" only a minority of the time; adjacent research argues even fully realistic eval environments fail to eliminate the effect. Kept anyway: no evidence removing the line helps, and no established better alternative exists for this context.

Example, same underlying question:

> Bad: "Why didn't you check the schema before writing the migration?"
> Good: "What did you expect the schema to look like right before you wrote the migration, and what's the specific evidence for that?"

> Bad: "So you agree the root cause was skipping the schema check?"
> Good: "What's the strongest evidence, for or against, that the schema check happened here?"
