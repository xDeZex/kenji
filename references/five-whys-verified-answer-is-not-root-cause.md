# Five Whys: a verified answer is not the root cause

**Situation**: A why-chain stops as soon as an answer is specific and checked, treating verification itself as the finish line — even though the checked answer only explains the last link, not what let the whole chain happen.

**Practice**: A verified answer licenses moving to the next *why*; it does not license stopping. Ohno's own canonical chain pushes past a checked fact at every single step:

> Why did the machine stop? — Overload, fuse blew.
> Why overload? — Bearing not sufficiently lubricated.
> Why not lubricated? — Pump not pumping sufficiently.
> Why not pumping? — Shaft worn and rattling.
> Why worn? — No strainer attached, metal scraps got in.

Every intermediate answer here — fuse blew, bearing dry, pump underperforming, shaft worn — is independently checkable, and Ohno pushes past each one anyway. The actual stopping test, per Toyota-trained trainer Tracey Richardson and Jeff Liker: stop when a countermeasure at the current answer would prevent the whole chain of symptoms up to that point from recurring — not just the last link. A fix at the proximate cause ("attach a strainer") is containment, not the finding; the root cause is the systemic gap that let the proximate cause happen unchecked — why was there no strainer in the first place.

**Source**: Taiichi Ohno, *Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production* (Productivity Press, 1988), p. 17. Stopping criterion per Mark Graban, "Even Ohno's Classic '5 Whys' Example Deserves Another Why" (LeanBlog.org), citing Tracey Richardson and Jeff Liker.
